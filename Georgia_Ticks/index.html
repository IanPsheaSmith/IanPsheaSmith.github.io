§ 01 · Overview

Ticks vector numerous human and veterinary pathogens, so characterising where they live — and which species live together — is foundational to disease control. At least 29 tick species have been documented in the country of Georgia, yet comprehensive studies of community composition and biogeography remain scarce, even as Borreliosis and emerging tick-borne viruses (Jingmen Tick Virus, the Tacheng Tick Viruses) circulate in the region.

This study analyses 720 tick occurrence records spanning 21 species across five genera, collected from 580 sites over 216 dates between 2013 and 2023. Combined with public climate, soil, terrain, vegetation, and land-cover data, the records describe tick geographies, biodiversity, community structure, bioregions, and species distributions across Georgia's diverse Caucasus terrain.

Tick diversity is high

Diversity across Georgia was strong (Gini-Simpson = 0.89), with rarefaction estimators (Chao1, jackknife, bootstrap) suggesting roughly two to six additional species are likely present but went undetected here. Haemaphysalis was the most represented genus, contributing seven species and 36.8% of records.

Three bioregions

Spatially-constrained hierarchical clustering (ClustGeo) over 25 k-means sites resolved three distinct bioregions echoing Georgia's broad east–west split around its mountainous centre. Annual precipitation, soil pH, and annual temperature range most sharply discriminated the regions. Bioregion 1 — the central grouping — held 84% of records and the highest richness and diversity, with D. reticulatus, Ha. punctata, and D. marginatus as significant indicator species.

Co-occurrence, not competition

Every species co-occurred with at least one other, and nine species pairs co-occurred significantly more than chance (Veech model) — all positive, none negative. The tightest pairing was D. marginatus with Ha. punctata (14 shared sites). The absence of negative associations suggests competitive exclusion is limited, with environmental filtering and shared habitat likely shaping community assembly.

Overlapping distributions

Six species with sufficient records (≥30) were modelled with boosted regression trees. All performed well (AUC and TSS > 0.75 across 100 replicates each), with soil organic carbon and annual temperature the most influential predictors. Suitability peaked in central and southeastern Georgia, and distributions overlapped strongly (mean Sørensen's coefficient = 0.61; up to 0.95 between the two Dermacentor species) — ground suitable for one vector often suits several.

§ 04 · Methods

How it was built

Distribution models

Species with more than 30 thinned presence records were modelled with boosted regression trees (gbm: 5,000 trees, interaction depth 10, learning rate 0.01, ten-fold cross-validation). Pseudo-absences were drawn at least 25 km from presences, matched one-to-one and down-weighted to 50%, then regenerated across 100 replicates per species; final maps average those replicates. Covariates below 1% relative influence were dropped stepwise, and pairwise range overlap was measured with Sørensen's coefficient.

Bioregions & clustering

Occurrence points were reduced to 25 sites via k-means (chosen by elbow analysis) to limit near-neighbour noise. Bioregions were delineated with ClustGeo Ward-like hierarchical clustering, balancing a haversine spatial matrix against a Jaccard environmental matrix at equal weight (α = 0.5). Indicator species were identified by multi-level pattern analysis (IndVal, 9,999 permutations).

Biodiversity & co-occurrence

Diversity was summarised with species richness, three rarefaction estimators, the Gini-Simpson index, and Hill numbers (q = 1, 2), with accumulation and extrapolation curves via iNEXT. Co-occurrence was assessed with both a count network and a probabilistic model (Veech), alongside kernel density estimation of sampling intensity. Analysis ran in R 4.5.1; data and code are on GitHub.

Public health relevance. Strong biodiversity, overlapping distributions, and the primacy of temperature and soil argue for integrated, multi-species vector surveillance. Where one medically-relevant tick is suitable, several often are — complicating single-species control and strengthening the case for coordinated, region-tailored programmes.
