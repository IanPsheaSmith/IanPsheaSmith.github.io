Working Paper

The Vulnerabilising Assemblage

Climate change, colonisation, conflict and corruption as synergistic vulnerabilising structures in Haiti

Overview

Process, not essence

This project examines how climate change, colonisation, conflict and corruption interact as components of a vulnerabilising assemblage in Haiti, drawing on Deleuzian assemblage theory and decolonial thought. Rather than essentialising Haiti or Haitians as "vulnerable," we analyse the structures and processes that actively produce vulnerability, particularly towards infectious disease.

Building on Wiesel and Power's (2023) conceptualisation, we adapt and extend the vulnerabilising assemblage framework into health geography and decolonial theory, using the cholera in Haiti as a case study.

Core argument: We call for viewing Haiti's complex vulnerabilities through the lens of assemblage theory, analysing how climate change, colonisation, conflict, and corruption interact to create systemic harm.

Framework

The four cogs

We describe four components (cogs) of a vulnerabilising assemblage within Haiti. Each vulnerabilises towards infectious disease through distinct pathways, but it is their interaction that generates the emergent properties of the assemblage.

IClimate Change

Exacerbates natural hazards, alters ecologies relevant for water-borne and vector-borne pathogens and disrupts sanitation and healthcare systems.

IIColonisation

Creates external dependencies, impoverishing structures and systemic inequities rooted in settler colonialism and neocolonial governance.

IIIConflict

Degrades healthcare and sanitation systems, generates displacement and diverts resources from public health.

IVCorruption

Erodes institutional trust, fuels vaccine hesitancy and corrodes systems that could otherwise reduce harm.

Theory

Assemblage theory and the vulnerabilising assemblage

Assemblage as machine

Assemblage theory, originating with Deleuze and Guattari (1987) and developed by DeLanda (2016), describes systems as multiplicities of heterogeneous components whose interactions produce emergent, irreducible properties. We treat the four structures as cogs within a vulnerabilising machine: interconnected components whose outputs feed into one another.

Wiesel and Power's six propositions

We mobilise and restate the six propositions of Wiesel and Power (2023), which describe how vulnerabilising assemblages operate:

  1. The assemblage operates principally through engendering susceptibilities to harm.
  2. The assemblage corroborates structures that permit harm and deconstructs those that prevent it.
  3. Vulnerabilising effects are not necessarily intentional, but they may be.
  4. Effects are often self-reinforcing, but they are not infinitely enduring.
  5. The assemblage is composed of heterogeneous components operating at multiple scales.
  6. The assemblage must be understood historically and contextually.

Three vulnerabilising mechanisms

Each cog may be linked to disease outbreaks through three distinct mechanisms: direct exposure to harm, the legitimisation of harm, and the erosion or deconstruction of systems of harm reduction and prevention.

Application

Case study: cholera in Haiti

Cholera in Haiti illustrates the operation of the vulnerabilising assemblage. All four cogs can contribute to the conditions under which an epidemic may emerge and persist.

Corruption generates vaccine hesitancy and systemic mistrust. Colonisation and neocolonial processes produced external dependencies and impoverished public health infrastructure. Conflict drives displacement, overcrowding in camps and overburdens healthcare systems. Climate change alters natural hazard dynamics, exacerbating conditions for waterborne disease transmission.

Highlights

Key takeaways

Climate change, colonisation, conflict and corruption synergistically vulnerabilise

Historic and present contexts in Haiti co-construct emergent and generative outcomes

Vulnerabilising assemblage theory is translated into health geography and decolonial thinking

Implications

Beyond static vulnerability

This framework moves beyond static vulnerability indices and essentialising language. By viewing vulnerability as actively produced through interconnected structures and processes, we open space for more historically grounded analyses of health disparities.

The vulnerabilising assemblage approach is not limited to Haiti or to cholera. It provides a transferable conceptual and methodological tool for examining how structural forces co-produce health inequities, and for grounding public health interventions in the material and political conditions that give rise to harm.
